# CONTEMPORARY-PROPS: THE CATALOGUE

339 candidates in, 306 unique after merging cross-domain repeats. 100 in the core, 150 in the follow-on, 56 cut outright, 33 merged into another candidate's generator.

## 1. THE CORE 100

Read top to bottom as the build order. Rows 1 to 32 exist to establish and prove the shared vocabulary (revolve profiles, extrusions and chamfers, linear and radial arrays, perforation), and each is cheap enough that a mistake in the helper is cheap to fix. Rows 33 to 69 compose that vocabulary into frames, carcasses and mechanisms, and are where the shared parts library (castor, hinge, drawer, door, louvre, slat) gets settled. Rows 70 to 100 are the expensive objects that should only be attempted once everything below them exists.

### Duplicate silhouette (17)

`desktop-nas-enclosure` reads as the rack chassis face at any distance; `information-kiosk-terminal` is the parking machine and self-checkout pedestal again; `channelizer-drum` is the traffic cone's banded family with a blunter top; `bike-storage-locker` is the skip and the cabinet with an arched lid; `food-vending-cart` is the pop-up canopy plus a counter plus wheels, all three already built; `tiered-produce-stand` is the gondola's stepped shelf profile; `tray-rack-trolley` and `iv-drip-stand` are the trolley frame and the five-star gas column with nothing added; `beam-seating` is the stacking chair shell repeated along a rail; `weight-plate-tree` is the barbell plate stack stood on a post; `dumbbell` (decor) is the loaded barbell short; `cable-reel` is the cable drum in a frame; `axle-stand` and `scissor-jack` are subsumed by the floor jack's job in a garage scene; `drum-fan` is the case fan cage scaled up on a yoke; `track-hurdle` is the work-zone barricade with fewer rails; `glockenspiel` and `djembe-drum` are the xylophone bar ramp and the drum kit shell; `trophy-cup` is the vase lathe family with handles; `cool-box` is the flight case as a tub; `shoe-rack` is a tilted version of the wall bar array; `rotary-card-file` is the cable drum at desk scale; `letter-tray-stack` is a shelf tier without a carcass.

### Needs a texture or a lit display to read (3)

`gym-scoreboard` is a blank wall panel until segments are lit and read as numbers, which is a display rather than geometry; `webcam` is a lump whose only distinguishing feature at any distance is the lens ring; `conference-speakerphone` is a perforated disc, and a perforated disc says nothing from more than two metres.

### Organic form code builds badly (8)

`artists-lay-figure` is a human figure however it is jointed; `vr-headset-stand` needs a sculpted head form to hold the visor; `macrame-hanging-planter` is a knotted cord net; `hiking-backpack`, `duffel-bag` and `golf-bag` are soft goods whose whole read is slump; `dome-tent` is a tensioned fabric skin over poles and will build as a lump; `pleated-curtain-rail` is cloth, and regular sine pleats look like corrugated metal rather than fabric.

### Not a thing, or a box with nothing to say (13)

`floor-cable-ramp` is closer to a floor covering than an object; `archive-box`, `vaulting-box` and `lever-arch-file` are boxes whose added structure does not survive at distance; `paper-trimmer` is a flat plate with an arm on it; `pencil-sharpener` is a small lump with a crank; `drawing-compass` is two sticks on a hinge; `wheel-brace` is an X of four bars; `bar-clamp` is a notched bar; `panelled-football` is a sphere, and seams do not change a sphere's outline; `metronome` is a blank truncated wedge; `hourglass` and `kettlebell` each have exactly one correct form with only a scale parameter, which fails the family test; `kayak` is one correct hull that would require a section-series loft used nowhere else; `citrus-press` and `tiered-cake-stand` are stacked plates and a lever, both already covered; `pottery-wheel` is a pan and a disc, carried entirely by the stool and pedal beside it; `filament-spool` ships as part of the 3D printer rather than as a prop; `alloy-road-wheel` belongs to a vehicle kit and on its own is a disc against a wall.

### Brand shaped (0 cut, 4 kept under condition)

Nothing was cut for this outright, because every candidate here is an archetype rather than a product. Four need an explicit rule at build time: `stand-mixer` must not take the tilting-head-plus-plinth proportions of the one famous example, `espresso-machine` must not reproduce a specific eagle-topped body, `single-board-computer` must not copy a named board's port stack and mounting-hole positions, and `capsule-vending-globe` must keep a plain pedestal. Generate the proportions from the parameter ranges, never from a photograph of one product.

## 4. THE SHARED VOCABULARY

### Geometry helpers the core module must provide

Ordered by how many of the core 100 depend on each.

1. **`box(size, chamfer)`** with per-edge chamfer radius and per-face inset. Used by roughly 80 of the core. The chamfer is not cosmetic; it is what stops the fridge, the microwave and the locker bank reading as untextured cubes.
2. **`lathe(profile, segments, sweep, wall)`** where `profile` is a polyline with per-vertex tangency and an optional wall thickness for open vessels. Rows 1 to 10 exist to prove it. Consumers: every vessel, every column, every bollard, cone, cylinder, tap, knob, foot and finial.
3. **`extrude(polyline, length, taper, endCaps)`**. The jersey barrier, every aluminium and steel profile, every slat, every rail, the gondola upright, the shelving angle.
4. **`sweep(profile, path, twist)`** with path from arcs and lines. Handles, spouts, bent tube frames, hoses, the swan-neck lamp arm, the trolley chassis.
5. **`tubeFrame(points, radius, bendRadius)`** as a specialisation of sweep that keeps a constant bend radius at every corner. The stacking chair, sack truck, garment rack, bike rack, step ladder, tripod legs. Without this the tube objects will have visibly different corner treatments from each other.
6. **`loft(sections, spine)`**. The extractor hood, the sofa arm, the guitar body, the wheelbarrow tray, the trolley basket taper.
7. **`dish(outline, depth, rim)`** for pressed sheet: the sink basin, the wheelbarrow tray, the seat pan, the bench seat shell, the litter bin hood.
8. **`helix(radius, pitch, turns, profile)`**. Springs on the task lamp, the cable drum winding, the vending machine coil, the shower hose, coiled cable.
9. **`arrayLinear(count, pitch, jitter, transformFn)`**, **`arrayPolar(count, radius, phase)`**, **`arrayGrid(rows, cols, pitch)`**. The three most-called functions in the kit. `transformFn` is what makes the size-ramp objects (gondola shelf depths, drawer heights, dumbbell racks) work without special cases.
10. **`perforate(target, pattern, holeShape, margin)`** supporting grid, staggered, radial-ring and slot patterns, with an inset rim so holes read at low angles. Mesh bin, colander, litter bin, locker louvre, radiator grille, PC front, speaker face, breadboard.
11. **`louvre(count, angle, depth)`** built on perforate, because slats-in-an-opening appears on six core objects with the same failure mode if hand-rolled each time.
12. **`toothRow(count, profile, set)`**. Saw, tape dispenser, ratchet, vice jaw, chain link, worm wheel.
13. **`lattice(bays, pattern)`** for truss and cross-brace. Scaffold, sign gantry, light tower mast, wall bars.
14. **`profileLibrary`**: rolled rim, ogee, plinth, waist, cove, quirk bead, chamfer stop. Named curves shared across the lathe and extrude callers so a rolled rim on a stockpot and on a litter bin are the same curve.
15. **`seededRng`** feeding jitter on pen splay, hanger spacing, slat gap, wear bevels, and colour variation. Determinism is the contract, so every consumer takes the rng explicitly rather than reading a global.

### Mechanism parts (shared, each owning its origin)

`hinge(axis, range)`, `slider(axis, travel, detents)`, `telescope(stages, collapse)`, `fourBar(links)`, `scissorLink(pairs)`, `ratchet(teeth, pawl)`, `screwJoint(pitch, travel)`, `castor(diameter, offset, swivel)`, `gasColumn(range)`, `spin(axis, decay)`. Thirty-three of the core hundred declare an action, and all of them decompose into these ten. Settle them before object 1.

### Material slots

The medieval kit's slots do not cover this kit, and the mismatch is specific rather than general.

**Carry over unchanged (5):** `wood` (workbench top, dining chair, bookcase, drafting board, piano case, guitar body), `steel-painted` (from `iron`: lockers, filing cabinet, shelving rack, barriers, tool bodies), `fabric` (from `cloth`: sofa, partition infill, canopy, whiteboard felt), `ceramic-glazed` (from `clay`: basin, toilet, mug, vase), `brass` (from `bronze`: trumpet, bunsen barrel, candlestick, valve trim).

**Carry over with a changed meaning (2):** `cord` narrows from `rope` to nylon and PVC (blind ladder cord, chime cord, pull cords, hose); `glass-clear` becomes a first-class slot rather than an occasional one, because 11 core objects (wine glass, bus shelter, microwave door, oven door, display case, cooler, shower cubicle, terrarium, vending machine, monitor, PC window) fail without it.

**Dropped (5):** `leather`, `thatch`, `bone`, `gold`, `wax` have no consumer in 250 objects.

**New, each argued from at least three core objects (11):**

| slot | argued from |
|---|---|
| `plastic-matte` | keyboard caps, wheeled waste bin, portable toilet, trolley shell, router body, tool housings; the single most common material in the kit and it must not look like painted steel |
| `plastic-gloss` | kettle body, toaster shell, laptop lid, appliance fascia; the specular difference from matte is the only thing separating a kettle from a bin at a glance |
| `rubber` | castor tyres, chair glides, tool grips, hose, cable jacket, wheelbarrow tyre, door seals; needed on 20+ objects and it is what makes the castor part read as a castor |
| `steel-galvanised` | fence mesh, cable tray, dish rack, shopping trolley, wire basket, wheelbarrow frame; the spangled zinc read is different enough from painted steel that sharing one slot flattens every wire object |
| `stainless-satin` | sink, stockpot, extractor hood, fridge front, espresso body, range trim; the kitchen collapses without it |
| `chrome-polished` | mixer tap, scissors, towel rail, stool column, cutlery, whisk, bar frames; a mirror slot, distinct from satin in its reflection behaviour rather than its colour |
| `aluminium-brushed` | 3D printer extrusions, monitor stand, laptop chassis, heatsink fins, rack rails, ladder stiles; extruded profile is the signature modern material and it is neither steel nor chrome |
| `concrete` | jersey barrier, planter, bollard base, cabinet plinth, barrier feet, worktop; grey and matte with an aggregate speckle, and it anchors every street scene |
| `glass-tinted` | PC side panel, shower screen, sunglasses of the display case, projector lens, cooler door in the dark; a separate slot because transmission is the parameter, and reusing clear glass with an alpha loses the surface |
| `paper` | printer paper stack, till roll, paper towel roll, book block edges, filter, literature rack contents; matte, near-white, zero specular, and it looks wrong rendered as plastic |
| `emissive` | lamp shade lining, traffic signal aspect, bulb, status pole, lava lamp, screen-on state; the medieval kit had firelight but not a switchable emitter with a colour parameter |

**One special case:** `retroreflective` for the traffic cone collars, barricade stripes and sign plates. It is arguably a vertex-colour treatment rather than a material, but it must not tone-map like paint, so give it its own slot and let it be a no-op in renderers that do not care.

That is 23 slots. Every object in the core 100 is coverable with at most 5 of them, and the median is 3.

## 5. WHAT WORRIES ME

### The three most likely to look wrong when built

**`modular-sofa` (94).** It is the only core object whose entire read depends on looking soft. Chamfered box cushions with piping ridges is the honest description of what the code will produce, and the honest prediction is a stack of rounded blocks on legs. The medieval kit has no vocabulary for a compressed mass, and none of the 93 objects before it develop one. Either build a `cushion(outline, loft, seam, compression)` helper deliberately, with a real crown on the top face and a slump at the front edge, or accept that the sofa is the one object where the kit looks like a kit. I would build the helper, because the ottoman, the mattress, the exam couch, the weight bench and the chair pads in the next 150 all need it too, so the cost is amortised across six objects rather than charged to one.

**`close-coupled-toilet` (92).** The pan is not a lathe and not an extrusion. It is a compound blend from a rectangular footprint through a swept bowl to a rolled rim, and every generated toilet fails at exactly the same place: the trap bulge on the side. Nothing else in the core needs a blend of that kind, so it will be a bespoke object dressed as a parametric one. It is worth keeping because a bathroom without one is not a bathroom, but I would build it late, build it from a section series rather than pretending it is a revolve, and give it exactly two shape presets rather than a continuous family.

**`potted-snake-plant` (96).** It is in the core because a kit of 100 objects with no living thing in it produces scenes that look sterile, and the snake plant is the plant whose leaves are genuinely stiff twisted tapered strips. The risk is that the twist plus the edge band plus the seeded lean produces green knives standing in a pot. The fix is per-blade curvature that varies along the blade length rather than a single twist parameter, and a blade cross-section that is a flattened lens rather than a rectangle. If the first build looks wrong, move it to the next 150 and ship the core with no greenery rather than shipping something that undermines every scene it appears in.

### The structural decision I would push back on

**Spreading the first hundred across all nine domains.**

The core 100 above covers 21 kitchen objects, 17 street, 15 office, 14 living, 13 tools, 9 computing, 4 retail, 4 decor, 3 school. That distribution came from asking which objects best establish the shared vocabulary, which is the right question for the code and the wrong question for the user. Nobody can build a shop with 4 retail objects. Nobody can build a classroom with 3. What they can build is nine partial rooms, each of which announces what is missing.

The alternative I would argue for: pick three domains, ship them complete, and let the vocabulary fall out of that rather than driving it. Kitchen, office and street would be my three, because between them they exercise every helper in section 4 (lathe, perforation, carcass, drawer, tube frame, louvre, extrusion, emissive, concrete, glass) and each produces a scene somebody can actually assemble on day one. That is roughly 35 kitchen, 33 office and 32 street objects, which is a hundred, and the follow-on 150 becomes three more complete domains rather than a thinning-out of nine.

The counter-argument, which is why I built the catalogue the way I did, is that vocabulary discovered inside one domain calcifies around that domain's needs, and the fourth domain then fights the helpers. Building `perforate` for the mesh bin, the colander and the fence panel in the same week is what keeps it general. Both positions are defensible. The one I would not defend is the middle, which is what happens by default: nine domains at 30 percent completion each, with the helper set shaped by whichever domain happened to get built first.

### Decided: street first, and street complete

Fifteen models in, the default had happened exactly as predicted. The spread was
five street, four kitchen, two tools, and one each of retail, office, living and
decor: eight domains at about a sixth each, and nothing anybody could assemble.

So the order above is now read domain-first, and the domain is **street** — all
seventeen rows of it before anything else is started. Three reasons, none of them
about the code being tidier:

1. It was already the deepest domain, so it is the cheapest one to finish.
2. The three helpers this kit has grown so far -- `planSweepGeometry`,
   `extrudeGeometry` and the slat-with-gap rule -- are all street and furniture
   helpers. The vocabulary had already leaned this way whether or not anybody
   decided it should.
3. Seventeen street objects is a SCENE. Barrier, bollard, cone, bench, table,
   sign, bin, fence, lamp, shelter, signal: that is a street somebody can build
   on the day they install the kit. What carried the medieval kit was not its
   model count, it was one flythrough of a market square, and the machinery for
   that already exists in this repository.

The counter-argument above stands and is not being dismissed: helpers discovered
inside one domain do calcify. The mitigation is to write each new helper against
the catalogue row that will reuse it NEXT, in another domain, and to say in the
helper's own comment which row that is. `perforate` is the first test of that --
it is wanted by the litter bin here, and by the colander and the mesh bin in the
kitchen -- and it should be built to all three or it should not be built.

**Street is now complete: seventeen of seventeen**, closing with the bus shelter
and the traffic signal. The mitigation held. Six helpers came out of the domain --
`perforate`, `louvreGeometry`, `wheelGeometry`, `splitRuns`, `extrudeGeometry`
and `planSweepGeometry` -- and every one of them names rows in other domains in
its own comment. The rule also did its other job, which was to say NO: the
signal's slant-cut visor has no second customer anywhere in the catalogue, so it
lives in the model that uses it rather than in core.

Next is a domain, not a scatter. Kitchen and office are the two arguments above;
kitchen is deeper and already has four rows built.
